Hands-on investigation

Labs

Work through short analyst scenarios, map behavior to frameworks, and explain what evidence would confirm or dismiss a hypothesis. Good incident analysis is not only about spotting the symptom; it is about proving the story from the telemetry.

Lab 1: Suspicious login sequence

A finance user signs in from a new country at 02:00. Minutes later, PowerShell runs and a scheduled task appears. This pattern is consistent with account takeover followed by execution and persistence. Analysts should review identity, endpoint, and process telemetry together.

  • Which identity and endpoint logs would you check first?
  • Would you treat this as valid account abuse, malware execution, or both?
  • What would distinguish a compromised account from a false positive?
  • Which containment action should happen first?

Reasoning: The sequence suggests a likely initial access or credential compromise, then execution via PowerShell, and possible persistence using a scheduled task. A proper response would isolate the host, reset credentials, and review logins, EDR, and network telemetry for lateral movement.

Lab 2: DNS beaconing

An endpoint resolves high-entropy domains at a regular interval and makes rare outbound HTTPS connections. This pattern often indicates command-and-control or data staging. Analysts should look for beaconing behaviors, domain reputation, and process lineage.

  • Compare interval, jitter, volume, and domain age.
  • Correlate DNS, proxy, and endpoint process telemetry.
  • Record the indicators you would block or hunt.
  • Which ATT&CK phases would you map to this behavior?

Reasoning: Regularly timed, low-variance outbound resolution patterns are common in malware beaconing. If the process is a user-mode scripting engine, a signed binary with unusual child processes, or a hidden service, the case becomes much stronger.

Lab 3: Lateral movement after discovery

During an investigation, an endpoint starts enumerating local accounts, then successfully logs into a different server over WinRM or SMB using a service account. The attacker may be in the discovery and lateral movement stages.

  • What evidence confirms remote service use and whether it is legitimate?
  • What would you look for in authentication logs and service account usage?
  • How would you prioritize containment to reduce blast radius?

Reasoning: Discovery followed by remote service authentication usually indicates the attacker has credentials and is enumerating the environment before moving deeper or collecting data. Analysts should look for unusual source-host pairs, service account logins, and non-standard remote administrative activity.

Investigation checklist

  • Confirm the user, host, service, and time window first.
  • Correlate endpoint, identity, network, and application telemetry.
  • Map observed behavior to a tactic, technique, and likely objective.
  • Decide whether the event is malicious, benign, or a misconfiguration.
  • Communicate the impact, confidence, and next steps clearly.

Lab notes

Keep a short investigation record for each scenario, including evidence, hypotheses, and next actions.
