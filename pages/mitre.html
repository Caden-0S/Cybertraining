MITRE ATT&CK

MITRE ATT&CK is a knowledge base of adversary behavior based on observed tactics, techniques, and procedures. It is widely used to classify attack activity, prioritize detections, and support incident response and threat hunting in a common language across security teams.

Core concepts

Tactics

A tactic describes the attacker’s objective at a specific stage of the intrusion, such as initial access, persistence, privilege escalation, discovery, impact, or exfiltration. Tactics answer the question: “What is the adversary trying to accomplish?”

Techniques

A technique is the concrete action used to accomplish a tactic, such as phishing, scheduled task abuse, credential dumping, or lateral movement using SMB. Techniques answer the question: “How do they do it?”

Sub-techniques

Sub-techniques offer more detail within a technique. For example, a broader technique like credential access may include OS credential dumping, brute force, or token theft. This granularity helps defenders map telemetry more precisely.

Procedure examples

ATT&CK also includes real-world examples of how adversaries apply these behaviors in practice. This matters because defenders do not just look for abstract ideas; they look for concrete sequences of behavior in their own environment.

Why it matters

ATT&CK gives analysts a common vocabulary for discussing intrusions and linking suspicious behavior to likely attacker objectives. It is used in detection engineering, breach analysis, hunt development, and interview prep because it connects alerts to the broader adversary lifecycle.

How defenders use it

Teams can map their telemetry to ATT&CK to identify gaps in coverage, prioritize controls, and explain why a behavior matters. A phishing email that leads to PowerShell execution and persistence can be described as a chain of initial access, execution, and persistence across multiple stages.

ATT&CK in practice

  • Detection engineering: identify which logs, process telemetry, and network events correspond to a tactic or technique.
  • Threat hunting: look for sequences such as credential theft followed by remote service logins and suspicious outbound connections.
  • Incident response: explain what happened in adversary terms and how the activity progressed through the environment.
  • Interview preparation: communicate clearly that tactics answer “why” and techniques answer “how” during security discussions.

Key takeaways

  • Tactics answer “why?”
  • Techniques answer “how?”
  • ATT&CK is useful for both detection engineering and analyst communication.
  • Good analysts map alerts to the broader attack lifecycle rather than viewing them as single stand-alone events.
